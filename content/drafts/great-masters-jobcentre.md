By Friday at five I need four people in paid employment, otherwise Denise will put me on a Performance Improvement Plan. This is a plan for improving my performance, drawn up by Denise, who spent yesterday morning trying to print a video.

I've been assigned the artists. Four of them. Their previous adviser is on leave, something to do with his stomach.

Vincent goes first. I ask him what he can do.

"I paint."

"Apart from that."

"I draw."

"Apart from related activities."

He thinks about this. His beard moves upwards. He can drive.

Now we're fucking talking.

***

The post office gives Vincent a van. On Tuesday I visit to check that he's settling in. He's painted the van, the hubcaps, and most of the windscreen. His route takes him through three villages and a field, although the field hasn't ordered anything.

![Van Gogh driving a blue and yellow postal van through a swirling Provençal landscape.](/assets/images/artists/vincent_van_gogh.png)

"Why the field?"

"It's beautiful."

"Is it on your route?"

"It is now."

He shows me the week's deliveries. Thirty seven letters, eleven parcels, all delivered. Each recipient has signed. I feel a great tenderness for this ginger man. He could have painted seventeen thousand yellow flowers and I wouldn't have felt what I feel now, looking at his signatures.

"Do you think they'll keep me on?"

"With figures like these, Vincent, absolutely."

"I'd like to buy some blue."

Everyone needs a goal.

***

Edgar gets a trial at the petrol station. It goes badly because he brings the dancers.

![Degas holding a petrol nozzle beside ballet dancers at a filling station.](/assets/images/artists/edgar_degastation.png)

"I said I worked with dancers. This was on my form."

"Your form said you were an independent artist."

"Yes."

"So why do you need six dancers to sell petrol?"

"Five."

"Edgar."

"The sixth is her mother."

The owner wants them off the forecourt. Customers can't tell who's serving, who needs serving, and whether the woman standing on one leg by pump three is having a medical emergency. She's stretching, Edgar explains, the same leg for forty minutes, which apparently requires both a mother and a full tank of unleaded.

The owner keeps Edgar on if he gets rid of the dancers. Edgar asks whether he can give them a reference. I tell him to call their adviser.

Two jobs. Wednesday. We're doing alright.

***

Claude wants to open a launderette. He's found premises, bought machines, done a business plan. It has a graph. Nobody else brought a graph. Vincent brought a potato.

Our scheme offers an employer four thousand pounds for each person recruited from my caseload. Claude intends to recruit Hieronymus, my fourth and final artist. He has a background in very detailed work involving large groups of people who aren't happy to be there. Commercial laundry, perfect.

I approve the application.

By Thursday Claude reports twenty eight thousand pounds in takings. The graph goes almost vertically upwards. I move my chair back to appreciate it.

I call Denise. Four placements, I tell her. Two salaried, one self-employed, one salaried by the self-employed. A beautiful little family of taxable endeavour.

"Has the employer paid Hieronymus yet?"

"Tomorrow."

"Then it's three placements."

"Well, three and a half."

"We can't submit half a person."

I delete the half person from the spreadsheet.

***

Friday morning. Claude's accounts arrive. Wages: nil. Detergent: £8.40. Takings: £43,600.

He charges six pounds for a wash. That's over seven thousand washes since Wednesday. I calculate this twice because I want to be happy about it.

I drive to the premises.

![Monet loading euro banknotes and water lilies into a washing machine.](/assets/images/artists/claude_monet_laundering.png)

"Claude."

"Good morning."

"What is that in the machine."

"Money."

"Why is there money in your machine."

"It was dirty."

He's wearing a straw hat indoors. I can't decide whether this makes things better or worse.

"Who brought it?"

"Customers."

"Which customers?"

"Paying customers."

"Claude, you've been trading for two days. How did these paying customers find you?"

He points to the sign in the window. LAUNDERING. DISCRETION. LARGE LOADS WELCOME.

There are flowers growing out of a pile of fifties. I hadn't covered this in the business plan review.

"And what are these?"

"Lilies."

"I can see that."

"They soften the water."

I ask to see Hieronymus.

***

Behind a curtain is a much larger room than the premises ought to contain. Machines stacked on machines, baskets, naked men, something with a beak ironing a pair of trousers. Flames emerge from what I hope is the boiler. A man is being fed into a dryer by two other men who also look like they could use some clothes.

![A Bosch triptych filled with monstrous washing machines, naked workers, ironing, and infernal laundry.](/assets/images/artists/hieronymus_bosch.png)

Hieronymus greets me and asks whether I brought whites.

"Where did all these people come from?"

"They've always been here."

"Are they employees?"

"Sinners."

"Is that a subcontractor?"

"They're here to be cleansed."

Behind him, a man with a funnel on his head pushes another man into a wicker basket. His legs hang over the sides and kick slowly. I look for a fire exit, find a small door marked FIRE, leave it.

"Look, Hieronymus, I need evidence that Claude has paid you."

"He hasn't."

"Has he said when he'll pay you?"

"When the work is done."

The basket goes past us again. Now there's a violin in it too.

"And when is that?"

"At the end of days."

***

Four thirty. I get Claude and Hieronymus together by the till. Denise has called twice. I haven't answered because I'm busy improving my performance.

"Claude, you need to pay the man. He's worked two days."

"He signed the contract."

"Did it say end of days?"

"Payment on completion."

Hieronymus nods. He thought that was fair at the time.

I explain that the four thousand pound grant is conditional on him paying wages. Claude says that this is why he needs the four thousand pounds. I explain that he has forty three thousand six hundred pounds of revenue.

"Those are the customers' banknotes."

"Those aren't takings?"

"We take six pounds."

He's done nine washes. Fifty four pounds. He's spent most of it on lilies.

Four forty seven. I take off my jacket.

"How much to clean this?"

"Six pounds."

"And to have Hieronymus watch it? For, say, an hour. A paid hour."

Claude looks at Hieronymus. Hieronymus looks towards the curtain. Something screams back.

"Hundred and twenty."

I pay. Claude pays Hieronymus. He signs. I photograph the receipt and send it to Denise at four fifty eight.

Four placements. My phone lights up with a thumbs up from the woman who can't print a video.

"Don't put it in the dryer," I tell Hieronymus. "It's wool."

He has already gone through the curtain. I follow him. There's my jacket, coming past in the basket, sleeves hanging over the sides.

"Can I have that back please."

The thing with the beak holds out a little numbered ticket.

"No, that's my jacket."

It points at a chair.
