title: The Pannelli Collection
institution: Fedfer Museum of Art
preview: "Ten works from the Pannelli Collection, presented together for the first time at the Fedfer Museum of Art."
---

The Pannelli Collection brings together ten works spanning five centuries of European art, presented here for the first time as a coherent ensemble. Developed through sustained dialogue between the Foundation and the Museum's research and conservation teams, the exhibition traces the relationship between artistic authorship and the material worlds through which it becomes visible.

These works have circulated primarily within private settings, where their proximity to everyday life has shaped the conditions of their reception. Their arrival at the Museum opens a space for considering how familiar artistic languages acquire new meanings through changing contexts of use, exchange, and display. We are grateful to the Foundation for making this encounter possible.

{{< catalogue-entry title="Vincent Van Gogh" year="1888" medium="Oil on canvas" provenance="Acquired from the Pannelli family garage" src="/assets/images/artists/vincent_van_gogh.png" alt="Van Gogh driving a yellow postal van through a swirling Provençal landscape, with a sunflower painting on its roof." >}}
The sunflower painting on the roof introduces a second pictorial surface into the landscape, staging the work's own passage between the site of production and an uncertain destination. The artist occupies the driver's seat, yet the brushwork refuses a stable distinction between the body that moves, the means of its movement, and the world through which it passes.

The postal vehicle brings authorship into relation with delivery. Both involve transmitting something intended for another, whose arrival can never be entirely guaranteed.

Previously catalogued as a picture of a delivery driver, the work was valued at €340. Its reattribution has opened a substantially different horizon of reception, including first-class transport accompanied by two couriers who were not permitted to sit near it.
{{< /catalogue-entry >}}

{{< catalogue-entry title="Edgar Degastation" year="1909" medium="Oil on canvas" provenance="Formerly displayed in the Pannelli service station" src="/assets/images/artists/edgar_degastation.png" alt="Degas portrays himself holding a petrol nozzle beside ballet dancers at a filling station." >}}
Degas places himself within the scene, holding the nozzle while the dancers attend to their bodies. This self-portrait relocates the artist from his familiar position as observer to an infrastructure of service: the hand that records movement has become the hand that supplies its energy.

His presence confers no obvious authority. The dancers remain absorbed in preparations whose duration appears independent of his willingness to assist. Self-representation becomes an enquiry into the limits of authorship within a scene that continues without acknowledging its maker.

Dr Vetter's observation that one dancer is simply tying her shoe offers a valuable account of what the image permits us to see. Our reading asks what such an account permits us to overlook.
{{< /catalogue-entry >}}

{{< catalogue-entry title="Candinsky" year="1926" medium="Oil on canvas" provenance="Pannelli pantry collection" src="/assets/images/artists/candinsky.png" alt="A tin can surrounded by angular planes, circles, and intersecting lines in yellow, blue, red, and black." >}}
Within a field of intersecting lines, the can establishes a volume whose apparent legibility is complicated by its refusal to disclose its contents. The surrounding geometry approaches this reserve through trajectories that touch its edge before being redirected elsewhere.

Professor Turpel's eleven-year study located the work's significance in the possibility of peas. His former student, Dr Grol, subsequently argued that none of his conclusions excluded beans, and that his confidence reproduced the demand for stable representation the painting puts into question.

Technical examination would alter the community of interpretation sustained by this uncertainty, including two academic careers and an annual conference. We have elected to preserve the work's capacity to generate knowledge.
{{< /catalogue-entry >}}

{{< catalogue-entry title="Francis Bacon Sandwich" year="1953" medium="Oil on board" provenance="Acquired with the contents of a Pannelli café" src="/assets/images/artists/francis_bacon_sandwich.png" alt="A screaming Francis Bacon enclosed between slices of bread and strips of bacon, beside a sixpence price card." >}}
The figure's enclosure brings the pictorial operations of framing and compression into an intimate relationship with the organisation of a meal. Flesh occupies the space of filling; the hands extend towards the plate's edge. The surrounding bacon renders a secure distinction between person and portion increasingly difficult to maintain.

The price card anchors this encounter in the café's social world. Sixpence assigns a value without clarifying which elements are included. The scream may express bodily vulnerability, refusal, or anticipation of the experience awaiting whoever ordered it.

A contemporary interpretation is available in the Museum café, at a price reflecting the costs of bread, bacon, and the institution within which this enquiry takes place.
{{< /catalogue-entry >}}

{{< catalogue-entry title="Paul Kleenex" year="1931" medium="Watercolour on paper" provenance="Gift of Mrs Pannelli, in memory of her first husband" src="/assets/images/artists/paul_kleenex.png" alt="Paul Klee emerging among white tissues from a patterned box, against a landscape of abstract shapes." >}}
The face appears where the tissue leaves the box, locating the individual's emergence within a structure designed to make the next individual readily available. The surrounding sheets suggest care, exposure, and the provisional character of an object intended to be removed and replaced.

Mrs Pannelli's dedication invites attention to the material supports through which grief becomes manageable. The tissue receives what the face cannot contain; the box reassures us of a considerable remaining supply.

A proposal to store the work in an acid-free box prompted two meetings concerning the status of enclosure and the implications of a second box. The committee resolved to maintain access to these questions through display.
{{< /catalogue-entry >}}

{{< catalogue-entry title="MC Escher" year="1962" medium="Lithograph" provenance="Formerly in the Pannelli nightclub" src="/assets/images/artists/mc_escher.png" alt="Escher holding a microphone amid impossible staircases, tessellated birds, a DJ, and crowds facing conflicting directions." >}}
The microphone establishes a recognisable economy of address while the architecture disperses the public to whom that address is directed. Each group occupies a position from which it may understand itself to be facing the performer. The image withholds a view capable of confirming these claims together.

The work's nightclub provenance lends force to its enquiry into access and proximity. From the performer's position, the crowd's remarkable distribution may suggest that everybody has been sold a restricted-view ticket.

A proposed immersive reconstruction was suspended after the fire inspector's attempts to identify a route of egress returned him to the entrance on four successive drawings. These are retained as documentation of the encounter.
{{< /catalogue-entry >}}

{{< catalogue-entry title="Géricault of Duty" year="1819" medium="Oil on canvas" provenance="Pannelli maritime holdings" src="/assets/images/artists/gericault_of_duty.png" alt="Shipwrecked men gaming on a raft, with monitors among the bodies and one survivor raising a console overhead." >}}
The raised console redirects the composition's appeal to a distant horizon. Below it, bodies occupy individually negotiated fields of action within a shared catastrophe. The sea threatens everyone. Several are waiting for a match to load.

Previously identified as signalling for rescue, the central figure is now understood to be seeking a stronger connection. Survival and participation become inseparable where continued participation requires a suspension of attention to the circumstances in which one's body has been placed.

Questions about the power supply deserve consideration. We also invite attention to the figure lying face down, whose withdrawal has made a monitor available to somebody else. Infrastructure includes these less visible contributions. The work is displayed in the east gallery, where the ceiling has extended its enquiry into exposure to the elements.
{{< /catalogue-entry >}}

{{< catalogue-entry title="Hieronymus Bosch" year="c. 1510" medium="Oil on panel" provenance="Pannelli domestic appliance division" src="/assets/images/artists/hieronymus_bosch.png" alt="A triptych of tormented figures among towers of washing machines, laundry baskets, and monstrous attendants." >}}
The promise of purification generates an expanding architecture of maintenance. Each machine offers completion; each surrounding basket defers it into another load. People carry laundry, enter machines, and emerge as burdens for other people. The triptych gathers these successive moments into a field whose scale remains unavailable to its individual participants.

A creature irons a garment belonging to someone absent. Its punishment is to work forever without seeing the finished pile. The Museum has enquired about its availability on Tuesdays.

Our sponsor's proposed reference to convenient home living prompted a discussion of the difference between reducing effort and reducing the task that remains. The agreed text refers to efficient home living. A bench is provided for visitors accompanied by children.
{{< /catalogue-entry >}}

{{< catalogue-entry title="Max Bill" year="1971" medium="Offset print on paper, 1:1 scale" provenance="Pannelli Swiss holdings, Zug" src="/assets/images/artists/max_bill.png" alt="A one trillion franc banknote designed in Swiss modernist style, wrapped in a spiral around the Matterhorn." >}}
Bill's design for a denomination the Swiss National Bank has not yet required wraps the Matterhorn in a single continuous gesture, binding together the nation's two most stable reserves. The mountain supplies the note's scale. The note supplies the mountain's valuation.

The work is too large to display and too valuable to insure. It is represented here by its certificate of authenticity, which the Foundation has kindly framed.

Enquiries regarding change should be directed to the Foundation.
{{< /catalogue-entry >}}

{{< catalogue-entry title="Claude Monet Laundering" year="1916" medium="Oil on canvas" provenance="On loan from the Pannelli Foundation" src="/assets/images/artists/claude_monet_laundering.png" alt="Monet feeding euro banknotes and water lilies into a washing machine in a sunlit laundrette." >}}
Light draws money, flowers, and machinery into a shared economy of colour. Denominations remain legible at the edges of forms already becoming petals. Monetary value encounters a surface on which value depends upon the conditions of looking.

What enters the machine retains a claim to continuity with what emerges, while acquiring different possibilities of circulation. The work invites consideration of the Museum's own role in receiving objects and preparing them for another field of recognition.

An allegorical reading has been proposed. The man may have left the money in his trousers.

The Foundation has offered to make the loan permanent, subject to our confirmation of its valuation. Our research and development teams are working towards an appropriate outcome.

The new roof is excellent.
{{< /catalogue-entry >}}
